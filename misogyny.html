Misogyny

Not only hatred toward women — but also the normalization of stereotypes, dismissal, objectification and unequal expectations deeply rooted in history and culture.

What misogyny actually looks like

Misogyny is often far more subtle than people imagine. It appears through comments, expectations, humor, beauty standards, social pressure and normalized double standards.

The most powerful forms of misogyny are often the ones society no longer notices.
  • women being interrupted more often
  • girls being valued mainly for appearance
  • female anger being labeled "dramatic"
  • expecting women to remain polite even when uncomfortable
  • criticizing confident girls as "bossy"

Historical roots

For centuries women in many societies were denied:

  • education
  • voting rights
  • financial independence
  • political influence
  • bodily autonomy

Women who challenged expectations were often punished or isolated.

Violence in history

Women throughout history were persecuted for stepping outside expected social roles.

  • witch trials
  • forced marriages
  • honor violence
  • criminalization of female independence

Many systems viewed women mainly through obedience and usefulness to men.

Childhood conditioning

Misogyny often begins subtly during childhood. Girls may grow up hearing:

  • "be nice"
  • "don't be loud"
  • "boys are mean because they like you"
  • "you should smile more"

Over time many girls learn to minimize themselves socially, fear being "too emotional" or prioritize appearance over confidence.

Meanwhile boys are often encouraged toward dominance and emotional suppression.

Internet culture & modern misogyny

Social media amplified misogynistic narratives through outrage-based algorithms.

  • "alpha male" influencer culture
  • appearance rating culture
  • ragebait podcasts
  • ironic misogynistic memes
  • humiliation disguised as humor

Because it is often presented as "just jokes", many people underestimate its impact.

Traditions & hidden origins

Some traditions are beautiful today — but originated from systems where women had less autonomy.

  • fathers walking daughters down the aisle
  • women taking husbands' surnames
  • expecting men to always "provide"

Misogyny vs Misandry

Sexism toward any gender is harmful. However, misogyny became deeply embedded into institutions and expectations for centuries.

Understanding this difference helps explain why historical inequalities still affect society today.

Why awareness matters

Recognizing misogyny is not about attacking men. It is about understanding social patterns that shape confidence, safety, relationships and expectations.

Awareness is not hostility.
